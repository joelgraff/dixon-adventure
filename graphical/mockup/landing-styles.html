EGA 16

Least art work · strongest retro

The fixed sixteen colors of King's Quest I–III. You can't blend colors, so the sky, the fog and the smoke break into checkerboard dithering. Flat shapes and hard edges are all you draw, which suits someone who isn't an artist, but it can read as crude next to anything made this decade. This one is converted automatically, which is its worst case: EGA artists chose every color by hand.

Limited 32

Moderate art work · pixel-locked effects

A hand-picked 32-color palette made for this river at dusk. Fog and smoke are pixels too, snapped to the same colors. This is the discipline that makes a Blender render look drawn rather than filtered, and it is the look many current pixel adventures settle on.

Modern pixel

Same art · more code

The same 32-color pixels, with smooth light and fog drawn on top at full resolution: the fire glows, the sun warms the haze, the mist drifts soft. The atmosphere comes from code rather than drawing, so it adds mood without adding art. The cost is compositing code and care that the effects don't smear the pixels.

How this was made

The scene is drawn with plain canvas shapes in full color at 320×180, then every pixel is snapped to the palette through a blue-noise dither, which scatters its dots with no repeating pattern. The buttons set how much: full, light (half strength), sparse (a quarter, so only the colors right between two palette colors get dots) or off. Anything that moves is never dithered, only snapped to flat palette colors: a fixed dither pattern shimmers when something slides through it. That is the same pipeline you'd run on a Blender render: render, shrink, lock to the palette, touch up by hand.

The scene is drawn in layers, back to front: the sky, far bank and river; the moving water, mist and the passing branch; the ferry; the near bank with the wagons, canoe and dock; then the people and the tar pot, sorted by where their feet touch the ground, so whoever stands further back is drawn first; then smoke; then the framing trees.

The walking uses a walk-area polygon, a single waypoint at the foot of the dock so you don't cut through the water, and depth scaling so you shrink as you walk out toward the river. That is roughly milestone one.
